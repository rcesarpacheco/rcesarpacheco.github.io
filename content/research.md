  <div class="paper-title">Priced Out: Financing Frictions and Startup Mortality</div>

  <div class="paper-abstract">Startups raise equity in a sequence of financing rounds, and most of them are led by an investor new to the firm, who bargains with its current owners over the share of the firm it receives for the capital it provides. We ask whether investors' bargaining power causes inefficient startup failures and, if so, how many. We model equity financing as a dynamic game and show that inefficient failures arise precisely when future investors have bargaining power. Bargaining power at the current round only changes how the round's surplus is divided. However, those who decide whether to fund the firm today treat the surplus that future investors will capture as a loss, so they let firms that are worth funding fail. Using PitchBook data on US venture-backed startups, we find that in otherwise similar rounds, owners keep 1.85% less of the firm when a new investor, rather than a current owner, leads the round. The model identifies this gap as new investors' bargaining power. Although small, it causes 7.1% of startup failures, and six in ten of these firms would otherwise go public or be acquired.</div>
</div>

  <div class="paper-title">When Does Disclosure Help? Information and Coordination in Equity Financing</div>

  <div class="paper-abstract" hidden>Policies such as mandatory financial disclosure for private firms change how much investors know about the firms they fund, and whether that knowledge is public or private. This paper asks when more information about firms raises welfare in the context of equity financing. I develop a model in which investors choose how much capital to contribute to each firm in exchange for a share of its future profits, observing only a private and a public signal of its productivity. How an investor's contribution responds to the contributions of other investors is governed by two opposing forces. Larger contributions by other investors raise the profits that investors share, which pushes an investor to contribute more, but they also dilute its share of those profits, which pushes it to contribute less. The balance between these forces determines whether investors react to one another too much or too little. I show that more public information can lower welfare when investors react too much to what other investors are doing, and that more private information can lower welfare when they react too little. Finally, if a policymaker can decide only whether information is public or private, not how much there is, it is optimal to make all information public or all of it private, and I characterize when each is optimal.</div>
</div>

### Works in progress

<div class="paper">
  <div class="paper-title">Bank Competition, Productivity and Inequality</div>
  <div class="paper-links">
    <button type="button" class="abstract-toggle" aria-expanded="false">Abstract</button>
  </div>
  <div class="paper-abstract" hidden>This paper studies how the distance between firms and banks shapes access to credit, entrepreneurship, and aggregate productivity. Using geolocated data on Brazilian bank branches and firms, I document that the share of municipalities without a branch and the distance between firms and their nearest branch have both risen over the past decade. I develop a heterogeneous-agent model in which agents spread across space choose between being a worker and being an entrepreneur, and entrepreneurs can borrow from a few banks at fixed locations. Distance affects credit through two channels. Firms pay a transportation cost to reach a bank, and this cost gives nearby banks market power, so part of the interest rate spread arises endogenously from competition among banks. Calibrating the model to Brazilian data, I find that removing banks' market power alone raises credit and output, but lowering transportation costs has larger effects. Distance also creates spatial inequality, as agents far from banks are less likely to become entrepreneurs and are worse off.</div>
</div>
